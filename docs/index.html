Fortune

John La Farge · 1901 · graphite
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

When commissioned in 1901 to make a stained glass window for the Frick office building in Pittsburgh, John La Farge produced this strange design of Fortune balancing precariously on a wheel—an emblem of the fickleness of fortune. The hazy, mysterious handling of the face of the central figure recalls contemporary French Symbolist painters such as Odilon Redon (1840–1916).

A Little Boy Lost

"Nought loves another as itself, Nor venerates another so, Nor is it possible to thought A greater than itself to know. "And, father, how can I love you Or any of my brothers more? I love you like the little bird That picks up crumbs around the door." The Priest sat by and heard the child; In trembling zeal he seized his hair, He led him by his little coat, And all admired the priestly care. And standing on the altar high, "Lo, what a fiend is here!" said he: "One who sets reason up for judge Of our most holy mystery." The weeping child could not be heard, The weeping parents wept in vain: They stripped him to his little shirt, And bound him in an iron chain, And burned him in a holy place Where many had been burned before; The weeping parents wept in vain. Are such thing done on Albion's shore?

William Blake · Public domain

✦ Read about this

William Blake was an English poet, painter and printmaker who went largely unrecognised in his lifetime but became a central figure of the Romantic Age. He produced his work using his own methods of illuminated printing, combining text and image, and developed a body of symbolically rich "prophetic works" that the critic Northrop Frye called among the least read in English poetry.

"A Little Boy Lost" belongs to Blake's broader concern with religious authority and the corruption of institutions. The poem stages a confrontation between a child's honest account of love—comparing his affection to a bird picking up crumbs—and a Priest who condemns him for setting "reason up for judge / Of our most holy mystery."

The child is seized, chained, and burned "in a holy place / Where many had been burned before." The closing question—"Are such things done on Albion's shore?"—turns the episode into an indictment of England itself, using Blake's recurring name for the nation.

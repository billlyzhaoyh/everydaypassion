Atten studier efter romerske draperifigurer

Melchior Lorck · 1551 · Pen, brunt blæk, pensel, brun lavering
SMK — National Gallery of Denmark · CC0

✦ Read about this

Melchior Lorck is the earliest Danish artist for whom both a substantial biography can be reconstructed and a significant body of work securely attributed. Of Danish-German origin, he worked across the Renaissance as a painter, draughtsman, and printmaker.

These eighteen studies after Roman drapery figures date from 1551 and were made in pen and brown ink with brush and brown wash. The subject reflects a standard practice of Renaissance training: studying antique sculpture and its handling of drapery to master the human figure and classical form.

Lorck is best known for a later undertaking — the most thorough visual record of 16th-century Turkish life and customs that survives, still a unique source for the period. The drapery studies belong to the SMK, the National Gallery of Denmark.

Today's poem

Song

I saw thee on thy bridal day-- When a burning blush came o'er thee, Though happiness around thee lay, The world all love before thee: And in thine eye a kindling light (Whatever it might be) Was all on Earth my aching sight Of Loveliness could see. That blush, perhaps, was maiden shame-- As such it well may pass-- Though its glow hath raised a fiercer flame In the breast of him, alas! Who saw thee on that bridal day, When that deep blush _would_ come o'er thee, Though happiness around thee lay, The world all love before thee.

Edgar Allan Poe · Public domain

✦ Read about this

"Song" appeared in Edgar Allan Poe's first collection, and like much of his early verse it is slighter and more conventional than the work for which he became famous. Poe (1809–1849) was an American writer, poet, editor, and literary critic, widely regarded as a central figure of Romanticism and Gothic fiction in the United States and of early American literature, best known for his poetry and his tales of mystery and the macabre.

The poem is a brief lyric in four quatrains, with the first and last stanzas echoing each other to frame the piece. It describes the speaker watching a woman on her wedding day, noting the blush that comes over her amid the happiness surrounding her.

What the speaker reads in that blush turns the poem: he suspects it may be more than maiden shame, and admits it has kindled a "fiercer flame" in his own breast — the jealous grief of a man who loves the bride he has lost.

Portrait of Dr. Louis Vintras

Alphonse Legros · 1904 · lithograph
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

Alphonse Legros (1837–1911) made this lithograph portrait of Dr. Louis Vintras in 1904. Legros was a French-born artist who moved to London in 1863 and was later naturalized as British, working across painting, etching, sculpture, and medal-making.

He was a significant teacher in the British etching revival, helping to train a generation of printmakers. The print entered the Cleveland Museum of Art in 1945 as part of the Mr. and Mrs. Lewis B. Williams Collection.

Today's poem

Later life

Something this foggy day, a something which Is neither of this fog nor of today, Has set me dreaming of the winds that play Past certain cliffs, along one certain beach, And turn the topmost edge of waves to spray: Ah pleasant pebbly strand so far away, So out of reach while quite within my reach, As out of reach as India or Cathay! I am sick of where I am and where I am not, I am sick of foresight and of memory, I am sick of all I have and all I see, I am sick of self, and there is nothing new; Oh weary impatient patience of my lot! Thus with myself: how fares it, Friends, with you?

Christina Rossetti · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Christina Rossetti (1830–1894 — though the facts here specify only that she was an English writer) produced some of the best-known devotional and romantic verse of the Victorian period, including the fantasy narrative "Goblin Market" and the sonnet "Remember."

This poem comes from her sequence "Later Life," itself a group of sonnets. The form here is a sonnet, built on a tightly limited set of rhymes, the b-rhyme sustained across both quatrains.

The poem turns on a foggy day that triggers a memory of a particular beach and its windblown waves. The remembered place is described as both "out of reach while quite within my reach," a paradox that opens into a passage of plain weariness: the speaker is "sick" of foresight, memory, possessions, and self, caught in an "impatient patience" with her situation. The closing line breaks the private mood by addressing friends directly, asking whether they feel the same.

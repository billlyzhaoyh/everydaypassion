Landscape with Cattle (recto)

Jules Dupré · mid- to late 1800s · brush and brown wash over graphite; framing lines in graphite
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

Jules Dupré (1811–1889) was one of the central figures of the Barbizon school, the group of French painters who worked in and around the forest of Fontainebleau and helped shift landscape away from idealized studio compositions toward direct observation of rural scenery.

This small sheet, made in the mid- to late 1800s, combines brush and brown wash over an underdrawing in graphite, with framing lines also drawn in graphite. Measuring roughly 16.5 by 26.1 cm, it treats the modest subject of cattle in a landscape, a recurring motif among Barbizon artists.

The drawing is in the Cleveland Museum of Art as part of the Norweb Collection (accession 1954.675.a). The notation "recto" indicates that the sheet also carries work on its reverse.

Today's poem

This is the land the sunset washes

This is the land the sunset washes, These are the banks of the Yellow Sea; Where it rose, or whither it rushes, These are the western mystery! Night after night her purple traffic Strews the landing with opal bales; Merchantmen poise upon horizons, Dip, and vanish with fairy sails.

Emily Dickinson · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Emily Dickinson wrote prolifically but published almost nothing during her lifetime; her work was largely unknown until after her death and is now regarded as canonical in American poetry. The Poetry Foundation credits her with creating "a distinctively elliptical language for expressing what was possible but not yet realized."

This short poem turns a sunset into a maritime scene. The sky becomes the "banks of the Yellow Sea," its colors the cargo of merchant ships that "poise upon horizons, / Dip, and vanish with fairy sails." The movement from "purple traffic" and "opal bales" to the vanishing ships traces the fading of evening light into darkness.

The poem is built in two quatrains, with Dickinson's characteristic compression and vivid, unexpected imagery. It treats the west as a place of "mystery," imagining the daily spectacle of sundown as an exotic commerce conducted just beyond reach.

Cann

Zachariah Brigden · c. 1778 · silver
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

"Cann" is an old term for a handled drinking cup, a form common in colonial American silver. This example was made around 1778 by Zachariah Brigden (1734–1787), a silversmith working in Boston.

The piece is raised in silver and stands about 13.4 cm high with its handle. It entered the Cleveland Museum of Art in 1940 as a gift of Hollis French.

Today's poem

From Paumanok Starting

FROM Paumanock starting, I fly like a bird, Around and around to soar, to sing the idea of all; To the north betaking myself, to sing there arctic songs, To Kanada, till I absorb Kanada in myself—to Michigan then, To Wisconsin, Iowa, Minnesota, to sing their songs, (they are inimitable;) Then to Ohio and Indiana to sing theirs—to Missouri and Kansas and Arkansas, to sing theirs, To Tennessee and Kentucky—to the Carolinas and Georgia, to sing theirs, To Texas, and so along up toward California, to roam accepted everywhere; To sing first, (to the tap of the war-drum, if need be,) The idea of all—of the western world, one and inseparable. And then the song of each member of These States.

Walt Whitman · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Walt Whitman, often called the father of free verse, is among the most influential poets in American and world literature. He blended transcendentalism and realism, and his 1855 collection Leaves of Grass drew controversy in his time, condemned by some as obscene for its sensuality.

"From Paumanok Starting" uses Paumanok, the Native American name for Long Island where Whitman was born, as its point of departure. The speaker imagines flying "like a bird" across the continent—north to Canada, west through the Great Lakes states and the plains, south to the Carolinas and Texas, and on toward California—pausing to sing the songs of each region.

The poem's project is to voice "the idea of all," a vision of "the western world, one and inseparable," before turning to "the song of each member of These States." Its catalogue of place names, its unrhymed expansive lines, and its reference to "the tap of the war-drum" mark characteristic features of Whitman's style and his nationalist ambition.

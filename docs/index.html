Wineglass and a Bowl of Fruit

Willem Kalf · 1663 · oil on canvas
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

Priceless objects crowd the corner of a stone table in Kalf's composition. Pieces like the silver platter, delicate glasswares, thick rumpled carpet, and a Chinese porcelain bowl with peaches and an orange not only represented the great wealth and global reach of the Dutch trading empire, but were also a way for the artist to demonstrate his skill in recreating various surface textures. Kalf used different kinds of highlights to render reflections—quick dots, lines, and dabs of paint—creating sparkling, twinkling light effects that subtly distinguish objects from the surrounding shadows. In 1797, the German poet Johann van Goethe wrote that Kalf's paintings lead to understanding "in what sense art is superior to nature and what the spirit of man imparts to objects when it views them with creative eyes. . . . if I had to choose between the golden vessels or the picture . . . I would choose the picture."

Did you know? Willem Kalf was the most accomplished proponent of the pronkstilleven , a Dutch term meaning "ostentatious still life."

Today's poem

When I was small, a woman died

When I was small, a woman died. To-day her only boy Went up from the Potomac, His face all victory, To look at her; how slowly The seasons must have turned Till bullets clipt an angle, And he passed quickly round! If pride shall be in Paradise I never can decide; Of their imperial conduct, No person testified. But proud in apparition, That woman and her boy Pass back and forth before my brain, As ever in the sky.

Emily Dickinson · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Emily Dickinson (1830–1886) was an American poet who was largely unpublished and unknown during her lifetime but whose work is now regarded as canonical. The Poetry Foundation credits her with creating "a distinctively elliptical language for expressing what was possible but not yet realized."

This poem turns on the American Civil War: a soldier returns "from the Potomac," his "face all victory," to the grave of his mother, who died when the speaker was young. The war intrudes directly in the central image of bullets that "clipt an angle" before the young man "passed quickly round" — a compressed, oblique account of his death in battle.

The four quatrains move from narrative toward speculation, questioning whether pride survives in Paradise, since no one living can testify. The poem closes with mother and son as an "apparition," passing "back and forth before my brain," fixed in the sky.

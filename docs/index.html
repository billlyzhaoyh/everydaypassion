View of Delphi with a Procession

Claude Lorrain · 1673 · Oil on canvas
Art Institute of Chicago · CC0

✦ Read about this

This painting of the ancient city of Delphi (in what is now Greece) presents a delicate rendering of the glowing Mediterranean atmosphere. Like many other foreign artists drawn to the ruins of antiquity, French painter Claude Lorrain was fascinated with the ancient Roman Empire. Here, he evoked a serene, bucolic world akin to the poetic vision of the ancient Roman writer Virgil, depicting the ruins restored to their original glory as Claude imagined. This painting was commissioned by Cardinal Carlo Camillo Massimi, an important and erudite collector in Rome.

In Drear-Nighted December

In drear-nighted December, Too happy, happy tree, Thy branches ne'er remember Their green felicity: The north cannot undo them With a sleety whistle through them; Nor frozen thawings glue them From budding at the prime. In drear-nighted December, Too happy, happy brook, Thy bubblings ne'er remember Apollo's summer look; But with a sweet forgetting, They stay their crystal fretting, Never, never petting About the frozen time. Ah! would 'twere so with many A gentle girl and boy! But were there ever any Writhed not at passed joy? The feel of not to feel it, When there is none to heal it Nor numbed sense to steel it, Was never said in rhyme.

John Keats · Public domain

✦ Read about this

John Keats was an English poet of the second generation of Romantic poets, alongside Byron and Shelley. His work was in publication for less than four years before he died of tuberculosis at 25. Indifferently received in his lifetime, his reputation grew rapidly after his death, and by the end of the nineteenth century he was firmly in the English canon, influencing the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood.

"In Drear-Nighted December" is built on a contrast between nature's unconscious endurance and human memory. The tree cannot recall its "green felicity," and the brook has forgotten Apollo's summer; neither suffers in the frozen season because neither remembers past warmth. The final stanza turns to people, who cannot escape grief over "passed joy."

The poem is arranged in three eight-line stanzas with a repeated opening line. Its close names a particular state—"The feel of not to feel it"—that, Keats claims, "was never said in rhyme."

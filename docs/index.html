Man and Two Women at the Sea Shore

Utagawa Toyokuni · 1769–1825 · color woodblock print
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

Utagawa Toyokuni (1769–1825) led the Utagawa school to the dominant position it held in Japanese printmaking through the nineteenth century. As its second head, he built the school into a commercial and artistic powerhouse; his success later prompted several of his students to take his art-name, so he is often called Toyokuni I to distinguish him from them.

Toyokuni worked in ukiyo-e, the genre of woodblock prints depicting the "floating world" of urban pleasures. He is best known for portraits of kabuki actors, though this sheet, *Man and Two Women at the Sea Shore*, depicts a shoreline scene.

The print is a color woodblock, produced by carving separate blocks for the outlines and each color and printing them in sequence onto paper. It entered the Cleveland Museum of Art in 1916 as an anonymous gift.

A Dream

Once a dream did weave a shade O'er my angel-guarded bed, That an emmet lost its way Where on grass methought I lay. Troubled, wildered, and forlorn, Dark, benighted, travel-worn, Over many a tangle spray, All heart-broke, I heard her say: "Oh my children! do they cry, Do they hear their father sigh? Now they look abroad to see, Now return and weep for me." Pitying, I dropped a tear: But I saw a glow-worm near, Who replied, "What wailing wight Calls the watchman of the night? "I am set to light the ground, While the beetle goes his round: Follow now the beetle's hum; Little wanderer, hie thee home!"

William Blake · Public domain

✦ Read about this

William Blake (1757–1827) was an English poet, painter, and printmaker who went largely unrecognised in his lifetime but became a seminal figure of the Romantic Age. He lived in London almost his entire life, apart from three years in Felpham, producing a symbolically rich body of work in both poetry and visual art. He regarded the imagination as "the body of God," or "human existence itself."

"A Dream" presents a small nocturnal vision: the sleeping speaker dreams of a lost ant (an "emmet") separated from her children, who grieves until a glow-worm, the "watchman of the night," directs her home by the beetle's hum. The poem works through plain quatrains in rhyming couplets, with a simple narrative frame.

Beneath its childlike surface, the poem turns on themes of loss, parental anxiety, and the consolation offered by the natural world's small guiding lights.

Titelvignet til et søkort: "Pass Kaart over Kattegatet"

J.F. Clemens · 1799 · Blyant
SMK — National Gallery of Denmark · CC0

✦ Read about this

This drawing is a design for the decorative title vignette of a sea chart, the "Pass Kaart over Kattegatet" — a navigational map covering the Kattegat, the strait between Denmark and Sweden. Rather than a finished artwork, it is a working study for a printed element, executed in pencil.

It was made in 1799 by J.F. Clemens, a Danish engraver active in the late eighteenth century. Title vignettes of this kind combined practical cartographic labeling with ornamental imagery, and the preparatory drawing would have served as the basis for an engraved plate.

The sheet is held in the collection of the SMK — National Gallery of Denmark.

To a Friend Who Sent Me Some Roses

As late I rambled in the happy fields, What time the skylark shakes the tremulous dew From his lush clover covert;—when anew Adventurous knights take up their dinted shields; I saw the sweetest flower wild nature yields, A fresh-blown musk-rose; 'twas the first that threw Its sweets upon the summer: graceful it grew As is the wand that Queen Titania wields. And, as I feasted on its fragrancy, I thought the garden-rose it far excelled; But when, O Wells! thy roses came to me, My sense with their deliciousness was spelled: Soft voices had they, that with tender plea Whispered of peace, and truth, and friendliness unquelled.

John Keats · Public domain

✦ Read about this

John Keats (1795–1821) belonged to the second generation of English Romantic poets, alongside Byron and Shelley. His poems circulated for less than four years before he died of tuberculosis at twenty-five, and were received indifferently in his lifetime; his reputation grew rapidly after his death, and by the end of the century he had entered the canon of English literature, strongly influencing the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood.

This poem is a sonnet addressed to a friend, named in the text as "Wells," who sent Keats roses. It opens with a pastoral ramble through summer fields and the discovery of a wild musk-rose, which the speaker judges finer than any garden-rose—until his friend's roses arrive and surpass it.

The sonnet turns on a comparison between natural and gifted flowers, with the friend's roses "whispering" of peace, truth, and friendship. Keats draws on Romantic nature imagery and a literary allusion to Titania, the fairy queen, in praising the gift.

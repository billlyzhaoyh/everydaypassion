Embers Glow

Theodore Roussel · 1890–1897 · color etching and aquatint
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

Theodore Roussel (1847–1926) was a French-born artist who worked in England, where he became known for landscapes and genre scenes. He made "Embers Glow" between 1890 and 1897 using color etching and aquatint, techniques that allowed him to build tonal and chromatic effects directly into the printed plate.

Roussel pursued color printmaking during a period when the medium was being actively experimented with and refined. The print is modest in scale, with a sheet measuring roughly 24 by 20 centimeters.

The impression at the Cleveland Museum of Art was given by The Print Club of Cleveland in 2013.

Adieu to a Soldier

ADIEU, O soldier! You of the rude campaigning, (which we shared,) The rapid march, the life of the camp, The hot contention of opposing fronts—the long manoeuver, Red battles with their slaughter,—the stimulus—the strong, terrific game, Spell of all brave and manly hearts—the trains of Time through you, and like of you, all fill’d, With war, and war’s expression. Adieu, dear comrade! Your mission is fulfill’d—but I, more warlike, Myself, and this contentious soul of mine, Still on our own campaigning bound, Through untried roads, with ambushes, opponents lined, Through many a sharp defeat and many a crisis—often baffled, Here marching, ever marching on, a war fight out—aye here, To fiercer, weightier battles give expression.

Walt Whitman · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Walt Whitman (1819–1892) was an American poet, essayist, and journalist often called the father of free verse, and one of the most influential figures in American and world literature. He blended transcendentalism and realism, and his 1855 collection Leaves of Grass drew controversy for its overt sensuality, condemned by some as obscene.

"Adieu to a Soldier" belongs to Whitman's body of Civil War poetry, drawn from experience he describes as shared "rude campaigning." In characteristic free verse, the poem bids farewell to a departing comrade whose "mission is fulfill'd," set against the imagery of marches, camps, and "red battles with their slaughter."

The poem turns on a contrast: as the soldier leaves the fighting behind, the speaker declares himself "more warlike," bound to continue his own campaign "through untried roads" and "fiercer, weightier battles." The soldier's war is over; the poet's struggle, waged by his "contentious soul," goes on.

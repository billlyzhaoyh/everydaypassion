Den barmhjertige samaritan finder den sårede

Wilhelm Marstrand · 1810-1873 · Blyant, pen og brunt blæk
SMK — National Gallery of Denmark · CC0

✦ Read about this

This drawing by Wilhelm Marstrand depicts the biblical parable of the Good Samaritan discovering the wounded traveler. It was executed in pencil, pen, and brown ink, a combination that allowed the artist to move between quick preparatory marks and more finished linear detail.

Marstrand (1810–1873) was a Danish painter and illustrator counted among the leading figures of the Golden Age of Danish Painting. Beyond his canvases, he produced a large body of drawings, often drawn from literary, historical, and religious subjects.

The sheet is held in the collection of the SMK — National Gallery of Denmark in Copenhagen.

Today's poem

To Ireland

Bear witness, Erin! when thine injured isle Sees summer on its verdant pastures smile, Its cornfields waving in the winds that sweep The billowy surface of thy circling deep! Thou tree whose shadow o'er the Atlantic gave Peace, wealth and beauty, to its friendly wave, its blossoms fade, And blighted are the leaves that cast its shade; Whilst the cold hand gathers its scanty fruit, Whose chillness struck a canker to its root. I could stand Upon thy shores, O Erin, and could count The billows that, in their unceasing swell, Dash on thy beach, and every wave might seem An instrument in Time the giant's grasp, To burst the barriers of Eternity. Proceed, thou giant, conquering and to conquer; March on thy lonely way! The nations fall Beneath thy noiseless footstep; pyramids That for millenniums have defied the blast, And laughed at lightnings, thou dost crush to nought. Yon monarch, in his solitary pomp, Is but the fungus of a winter day That thy light footstep presses into dust. Thou art a conqueror, Time; all things give way Before thee but the 'fixed and virtuous will'; The sacred sympathy of soul which was When thou wert not, which shall be when thou perishest. ...

Percy Bysshe Shelley · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Percy Bysshe Shelley (one of the major English Romantic poets) was a radical in his poetry as well as in his political and social views. He did not achieve fame during his lifetime, but recognition of his work grew steadily after his death, and he became an important influence on later poets including Robert Browning, Algernon Charles Swinburne, Thomas Hardy, and W. B. Yeats. The critic Harold Bloom called him "a superb craftsman, a lyric poet without rival, and surely one of the most advanced sceptical intellects ever to write a poem."

"To Ireland" addresses Erin directly, lamenting the "injured isle" and the blight that has come upon it. The poem turns from Ireland's shores to a meditation on Time, personified as a giant and conqueror who crushes pyramids, monarchs, and nations alike. Against this destroyer the poem sets one thing that endures: the "fixed and virtuous will" and the sympathy of soul that predates and outlasts Time itself.

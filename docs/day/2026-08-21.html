Portrait of a woman as a shepherdess

Crispijn de Passe the Younger · 1635-40 · Metalpoint on prepared parchment (tafelet), inset into a nineteenth-century painted mount
The Met · CC0

✦ Read about this

This small portrait was made in metalpoint—a demanding technique in which the artist draws with a metal stylus on specially prepared ground, leaving marks that cannot be erased. The medium was largely archaic by the 1630s, which makes de Passe's use of it here notable. The support is a prepared parchment tablet, or tafelet, now set into a painted mount added in the nineteenth century.

Crispijn de Passe the Younger (ca. 1594–1670) worked as an engraver, draughtsman, and print publisher during the Dutch Golden Age. He belonged to the prolific Van de Passe family of printmakers and was the son of the engraver and publisher Crispijn van de Passe the Elder.

Made around 1635–40, the drawing depicts a woman in the guise of a shepherdess, a pastoral conceit popular in Dutch portraiture of the period. It measures only about 8.5 by 6.8 centimeters.

Today's poem

Dust of Snow

The way a crow Shook down on me The dust of snow From a hemlock tree Has given my heart A change of mood And saved some part Of a day I had rued.

Robert Frost · 1923 · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Robert Frost was an American poet known for his realistic depictions of rural life and his command of American colloquial speech. He frequently drew on settings from rural New England in the early 20th century, using them to examine complex social and philosophical themes.

"Dust of Snow" is a compact example of that method: a single incident from the natural world opens onto an interior shift. In two short stanzas, a crow shaking snow from a hemlock tree lifts the speaker's mood and rescues part of a day he had regretted.

The poem's form is tightly economical—two quatrains built as a single sentence, with short lines and simple rhyme. Its plainness of diction reflects Frost's characteristic use of everyday speech, and it turns an ordinary outdoor moment into a statement about how small things can alter a person's frame of mind.

Hjorte i en skov

Elias Meyer · 1807 · Olie på lærred
SMK — National Gallery of Denmark · CC0

✦ Read about this

Elias Meyer painted *Hjorte i en skov* ("Deer in a Forest") in 1807, working in oil on canvas. The work is held by the National Gallery of Denmark (Statens Museum for Kunst, SMK) in Copenhagen.

The painting dates from the early nineteenth century, a period when forest and animal subjects were a recognized genre in Danish landscape art. Its combination of woodland setting and wildlife places it within the pastoral and naturalistic traditions of the time.

Lines Written in Early Spring

I heard a thousand blended notes, While in a grove I sate reclined, In that sweet mood when pleasant thoughts Bring sad thoughts to the mind. To her fair works did Nature link The human soul that through me ran; And much it grieved my heart to think What man has made of man. Through primrose tufts, in that green bower, The periwinkle trailed its wreaths; And 'tis my faith that every flower Enjoys the air it breathes. The birds around me hopped and played, Their thoughts I cannot measure:-- But the least motion which they made It seemed a thrill of pleasure. The budding twigs spread out their fan, To catch the breezy air; And I must think, do all I can, That there was pleasure there. If this belief from heaven be sent, If such be Nature's holy plan, Have I not reason to lament What man has made of man?

William Wordsworth · Public domain

✦ Read about this

William Wordsworth was an English Romantic poet who, with Samuel Taylor Coleridge, helped launch the Romantic Age in English literature through their joint publication *Lyrical Ballads* (1798). "Lines Written in Early Spring" appeared in that volume.

The poem is built from six quatrains in a simple ballad-like measure, with alternating rhymes and a plain, direct diction characteristic of the collection. The speaker sits reclined in a grove, listening to birdsong and observing primroses, periwinkle, and budding twigs.

Its central movement is the tension named in the opening stanza: the pleasant scene brings sad thoughts. Wordsworth links the human soul to nature's works, and from the apparent pleasure he perceives in flowers, birds, and twigs he draws a contrast with human conduct. The refrain lamenting "What man has made of man" frames the poem's observation of nature as a reproach to human cruelty and social wrong.

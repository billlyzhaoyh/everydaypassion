Johan Thomas Lundbye · 1845 · Blyant, pen og gråt blæk
SMK — National Gallery of Denmark · CC0

✦ Read about this

This sheet of studies by Johan Thomas Lundbye (1845) combines several drawings in pencil, pen and grey ink: a cavalryman on horseback seen from the front and the back, together with a separate study of a horse's hind legs. The multiple viewpoints reflect a working method aimed at understanding the animal's anatomy and movement from all sides.

Lundbye was a Danish painter and graphic artist best known for his animal and landscape subjects. He was among the leading national romantic painters of his generation, alongside P. C. Skovgaard and Lorenz Frølich, and regularly depicted the landscape of Zealand.

His art responded to Niels Laurits Høyen's call for a nationalistic art rooted in Denmark's characteristic landscapes, historical buildings and monuments, and its rural people. The drawing is held by the SMK — National Gallery of Denmark.

A Birthday

My heart is like a singing bird Whose nest is in a water'd shoot; My heart is like an apple-tree Whose boughs are bent with thickset fruit; My heart is like a rainbow shell That paddles in a halcyon sea; My heart is gladder than all these Because my love is come to me. Raise me a dais of silk and down; Hang it with vair and purple dyes; Carve it in doves and pomegranates, And peacocks with a hundred eyes; Work it in gold and silver grapes, In leaves and silver fleurs-de-lys; Because the birthday of my life Is come, my love is come to me.

Christina Rossetti · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Christina Rossetti (1830–1894, though dates are not among the facts here) was an English poet known for romantic and devotional verse, including the narrative poem "Goblin Market" and the sonnet "Remember." She was among the most accomplished English poets of her period, equally at home in religious meditation and lyric expression.

"A Birthday" belongs to her love poems. It is built in two eight-line stanzas. The first piles up similes for the speaker's joy — a singing bird, a fruit-laden apple tree, a rainbow shell on a calm sea — each declared insufficient beside the gladness of love's arrival. The second stanza shifts from natural images to lavish, crafted ornament: a dais of silk and down, carvings of doves and pomegranates, peacocks, gold and silver grapes, fleurs-de-lys.

The "birthday" of the title is figurative: the coming of love marks the beginning of the speaker's true life, celebrated in rising intensity across both stanzas.

This plaster cast reproduces an ancient sculpture known as the Medici Faun, a satyr depicted playing a foot-operated drum and cymbals. The figure belongs to a group traditionally titled "Invitation to the Dance," in which the satyr is shown making music, his body arranged to suggest rhythmic movement.

The cast is by an unknown maker and is held in the collection of the National Gallery of Denmark (SMK). Plaster casts of this kind were produced to make celebrated antique and classical sculptures available for study and display far from their originals, serving as teaching tools in art academies and as collection pieces in their own right.

As a gipsafstøbning, the work was formed by taking a mould from an existing sculpture and casting it in plaster, allowing a faithful reproduction of the original's surface and form.

A Thing of Beauty (Endymion)

A thing of beauty is a joy for ever: Its lovliness increases; it will never Pass into nothingness; but still will keep A bower quiet for us, and a sleep Full of sweet dreams, and health, and quiet breathing. Therefore, on every morrow, are we wreathing A flowery band to bind us to the earth, Spite of despondence, of the inhuman dearth Of noble natures, of the gloomy days, Of all the unhealthy and o'er-darkn'd ways Made for our searching: yes, in spite of all, Some shape of beauty moves away the pall From our dark spirits. Such the sun, the moon, Trees old and young, sprouting a shady boon For simple sheep; and such are daffodils With the green world they live in; and clear rills That for themselves a cooling covert make 'Gainst the hot season; the mid-forest brake, Rich with a sprinkling of fair musk-rose blooms: And such too is the grandeur of the dooms We have imagined for the mighty dead; An endless fountain of immortal drink, Pouring unto us from the heaven's brink.

John Keats · Public domain

✦ Read about this

These lines open *Endymion*, John Keats's long poem, and contain his most quoted single line: "A thing of beauty is a joy for ever." Keats was an English poet of the second generation of Romantics, alongside Byron and Shelley. His poems had been in print for less than four years when he died of tuberculosis at 25, and they were indifferently received in his lifetime. His reputation grew rapidly after his death, and by the end of the century he was firmly in the canon of English literature, influencing the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood.

The passage is written in rhyming couplets. It argues that beauty is permanent and sustaining: its loveliness never passes into nothingness but offers quiet and "sweet dreams" against despondence and "gloomy days." Keats catalogues sources of such beauty — the sun and moon, trees, daffodils, streams, musk-roses — and extends it to the imagined grandeur of the "mighty dead," describing beauty as "an endless fountain of immortal drink."

Selvportræt

Ubekendt · 1642-1799 · Radering
SMK — National Gallery of Denmark · CC0

✦ Read about this

This etched self-portrait, held by the National Gallery of Denmark (SMK), is of uncertain authorship — the artist is recorded simply as unknown. It dates broadly to the period between 1642 and 1799, a span that places it within the long tradition of printed self-representation that flourished in European art from the seventeenth through the eighteenth centuries.

The work is an etching, a printmaking technique in which lines are bitten into a metal plate with acid before being inked and printed. Etching allowed artists a fluid, drawing-like freedom and was widely used for self-portraiture, a genre that let printmakers study their own features and demonstrate technical skill.

With the maker unidentified, the sheet is catalogued by subject and medium rather than attribution, a common situation for older prints whose signatures or documentation have not survived.

Remorse is memory awake

Remorse is memory awake, Her companies astir, -- A presence of departed acts At window and at door. It's past set down before the soul, And lighted with a match, Perusal to facilitate Of its condensed despatch. Remorse is cureless, -- the disease Not even God can heal; For 't is his institution, -- The complement of hell.

Emily Dickinson · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Emily Dickinson wrote prolifically but published almost nothing during her lifetime; fewer than a dozen of her poems appeared in print before her death, and her reputation rests on work discovered and issued afterward. Now considered canonical, she is described by the Poetry Foundation as having "created in her writing a distinctively elliptical language for expressing what was possible but not yet realized."

"Remorse is memory awake" is a compact three-stanza definition poem, a form Dickinson used often: it opens by naming an abstraction and then elaborates it through concrete images. Here remorse is figured as memory roused to activity, with past deeds appearing "at window and at door" like unwelcome visitors, and the soul made to reread a "condensed despatch" of its own history.

The final stanza presses the idea to a theological conclusion, calling remorse "cureless" and beyond even God's healing because it is "his institution, -- / The complement of hell."

Medal (reverse)

Jules Prosper Legastelois · 1914 · bronze
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

This small bronze medal, measuring just 7 centimeters across, shows the reverse of a work by the French medalist Jules Prosper Legastelois (1855–1931), made in 1914.

The object entered the Cleveland Museum of Art in 1919 as a gift from Lawrence Hitchcock. Its catalogue designation (1919.89.b) indicates that it is one side of a two-sided medal, the reverse being recorded here as a distinct component.

Produced in bronze, a standard material for commemorative and decorative medals of the period, the work dates to the first year of the First World War.

The murmur of a bee

The murmur of a bee A witchcraft yieldeth me. If any ask me why, 'T were easier to die Than tell. The red upon the hill Taketh away my will; If anybody sneer, Take care, for God is here, That's all. The breaking of the day Addeth to my degree; If any ask me how, Artist, who drew me so, Must tell!

Emily Dickinson · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Emily Dickinson wrote prolifically but published almost nothing in her lifetime; her work was largely unknown until after her death and is now regarded as canonical. The Poetry Foundation credits her with creating "a distinctively elliptical language for expressing what was possible but not yet realized."

"The murmur of a bee" is characteristic of that ellipsis. Across three compact stanzas, the speaker names ordinary natural phenomena — a bee's hum, the red of sunset on a hill, the breaking of day — and admits she cannot explain the intense, almost involuntary responses they produce in her. Each stanza turns on a refusal or deferral: it were "easier to die / Than tell," and finally the answer belongs not to her but to the "Artist, who drew me so."

The poem is built on tight rhyming couplets closed by abrupt short lines ("Than tell," "That's all"), a terse structure that enacts the very reticence it describes.

Parti i Falkoneralléen

Christen Købke · 1845-1847 · Blyant
SMK — National Gallery of Denmark · CC0

✦ Read about this

Christen Købke (1810–1848) died at 37, making this drawing from his final years one of his late works. He is counted among the leading figures of the Golden Age of Danish Painting, a period of concentrated artistic activity in Copenhagen in the first half of the nineteenth century.

"Parti i Falkoneralléen" was made between 1845 and 1847 in pencil (blyant). The subject is a view along Falkonerallé, a thoroughfare on the outskirts of Copenhagen. Købke often worked near his home and in the surroundings of the city, favoring unremarkable local motifs over grand or exotic subjects.

The drawing is held by the SMK — National Gallery of Denmark in Copenhagen.

Today's poem

Spain 1873–’74

OUT of the murk of heaviest clouds, Out of the feudal wrecks, and heap’d-up skeletons of kings, Out of that old entire European debris—the shatter’d mummeries, Ruin’d cathedrals, crumble of palaces, tombs of priests, Lo! Freedom’s features, fresh, undimm’d, look forth—the same immortal face looks forth; (A glimpse as of thy mother’s face, Columbia, A flash significant as of a sword, Beaming towards thee.) Nor think we forget thee, Maternal; Lag’d’st thou so long? Shall the clouds close again upon thee? Ah, but thou hast Thyself now appear’d to us—we know thee; Thou hast given us a sure proof, the glimpse of Thyself; Thou waitest there, as everywhere, thy time.

Walt Whitman · Public domain

✦ Read about this

Walt Whitman, often called the father of free verse, was among the most influential poets in American and world literature. He combined transcendentalism and realism in his work, most famously in Leaves of Grass (1855), which some denounced as obscene for its sensuality.

This short poem responds to political upheaval in Spain during 1873–74, the period of the short-lived First Spanish Republic. Whitman reads these events as the reappearance of "Freedom"—a figure he addresses as maternal—emerging from the ruins of Europe's feudal and religious past: wrecked kings, crumbling cathedrals, and tombs of priests.

Written in Whitman's characteristic free verse, the poem links Spain's revolutionary moment to "Columbia," his personification of the United States, suggesting that liberty is a single immortal force appearing across nations. The closing lines strike a note of patient confidence: freedom "waitest there, as everywhere," biding its time.

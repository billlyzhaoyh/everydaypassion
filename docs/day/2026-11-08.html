Camera Work: Portrait - Mrs. Harrington Mann

Clarence H. White · 1908 · photogravure
Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0

✦ Read about this

Clarence H. White was entirely self-taught in photography, taking it up only after visiting the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893. Within a few years he was internationally known for pictorial images that reflected the sentiment and social life of rural America, where he grew up in small-town Ohio.

This photogravure portrait of Mrs. Harrington Mann dates to 1908, two years after White moved from Ohio to New York City to be closer to Alfred Stieglitz and his circle. It was published in Camera Work, the influential journal through which Stieglitz promoted photography as a fine art.

White was a founding member of the Photo-Secession, the movement that argued for photography's status as a true artistic medium. He later founded the Clarence H. White School of Photography in 1914, the first American institution to teach photography as art.

The Lamb

Little Lamb, who made thee Dost thou know who made thee, Gave thee life, and bid thee feed By the stream and o'er the mead; Gave thee clothing of delight, Softest clothing, woolly, bright; Gave thee such a tender voice, Making all the vales rejoice? Little Lamb, who made thee? Dost thou know who made thee? Little Lamb, I'll tell thee; Little Lamb, I'll tell thee: He is called by thy name, For He calls Himself a Lamb He is meek, and He is mild, He became a little child. I a child, and thou a lamb, We are called by His name. Little Lamb, God bless thee! Little Lamb, God bless thee!

William Blake · Public domain

✦ Read about this

William Blake (1757–1827) was an English poet, painter and printmaker who went largely unrecognised in his own lifetime but is now regarded as a seminal figure of the Romantic Age in both poetry and visual art. He lived in London nearly his whole life, except for three years in Felpham, and produced a symbolically rich body of work that treated the imagination as "the body of God."

"The Lamb" belongs to Blake's work in the Romantic period and takes the form of a two-stanza poem built on question and answer. The first stanza poses the question of who made the lamb; the second supplies the answer. Its language is deliberately simple, with repeated refrains and a child's address to the animal.

The poem identifies the lamb's maker with Christ, who "calls Himself a Lamb" and "became a little child," linking child, lamb, and God through shared meekness and a shared name.
